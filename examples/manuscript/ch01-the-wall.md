# One — The Wall

## The gate at dusk

Aldric came up the inner stair two at a time, the way he had at twenty, and regretted it
at the top. The wind off the pass took his cloak and held it out behind him like a banner
nobody had asked him to raise.

Marrow was a wall with a town behind it, and everyone who lived here said so first, as
though the saying made it bearable. From the parapet he could see the road bend north into
the Vashen Heartland and vanish. Somewhere down that road, a man he had just sworn an oath
to was deciding how many years he could be made to wait.

"They'll come in spring," said the captain of the gate, who had said the same thing every
spring for six years.

"They'll come when the passes are dry," Aldric said. "Spring has nothing to do with it."

Below him the town made its evening noises. Behind the town, thirty miles south and eight
hundred feet down, the Vale went on being the reason any of this mattered — the barley,
the water, the four thousand people who had never once been asked whether they wanted a
duke. Marrow was the last thing standing between them and the road.

He stayed until the light went. It was the only hour of the day when the wall looked like
something other than an argument he was losing.

## Word from the Vale

Eight years earlier, and a hundred miles south, his mother had read a letter three times
and then walked out into the barley to think about it.

The letter had come from Marrow, which meant it had come from her son, who was
twenty-seven and posted to the coldest wall in the Duchy of Corrath because he had asked
to be. Maren had let him go on the theory that a young man who wants a wall should be
given one before he starts wanting something worse.

"He says the Vashen surveyors were on the pass again," said her steward.

"He says it every autumn."

"He says there were forty of them."

Maren folded the letter along its old creases. Corrath had held this valley for three
hundred years by being too much trouble to take and too poor to be worth it, and the
second half of that had stopped being true about the time somebody in the north worked out
what a granary was for.

Outside, the Vale was doing what it did in Seedfall — being green in a way that made the
whole argument obvious to anyone who stood in it for an afternoon. She had stood in it for
fifty years. She did not need the letter to know how this ended. She needed it to know how
long they had.
